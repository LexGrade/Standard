## MEMORANDUM OF LAW

### Preliminary Statement

Plaintiff Leslie A. Sample moves under Domestic Relations Law § 237(a) and
§ 237(d) for interim counsel fees of $15,000 and for an order directing
Defendant to advance the full $12,500 retainer of the neutral appraiser the
parties agree is needed to value Defendant's business.

The application turns on undisputed documents. Defendant's own produced tax
returns show K-1 income of $175,000, $212,000, and $240,000 over the last
three tax years (Exhibits C, D, E). Plaintiff earns $31,200. Defendant paid
his counsel a $25,000 retainer from his corporation's operating account
(Exhibit F); Plaintiff, with $4,300 in savings, has been quoted retainers of
$10,000 to $15,000 she cannot pay (Exhibit G). Defendant's counsel has
demanded that Plaintiff front $6,250 toward the appraisal (Exhibit A) — a sum
larger than everything she has.

The relief sought is deliberately modest: a first interim fee award at the
bottom of the quoted retainer range, without prejudice, and an *advance* — not
a final allocation — of the appraisal cost, expressly subject to reallocation
in the final judgment. This motion is the vehicle the statute itself
prescribes for exactly this situation; no alternative (waiting for trial, or
absorbing the costs) would secure the relief, for the reasons developed in
Points II and III.

### Legal Standard

DRL § 237(a) authorizes the court, in a matrimonial action, to direct either
spouse to pay counsel fees to enable the other to carry on or defend the
action, having regard to the circumstances of the case and of the respective
parties. Since 2010 the statute has contained an express burden-allocating
presumption: "There shall be a rebuttable presumption that counsel fees shall
be awarded to the less monied spouse." DRL § 237(a). The text thus does two
things: it makes the *relative* financial positions of the spouses the
operative comparison, and it places the burden of rebutting the presumption on
the monied spouse once the disparity is shown. Plaintiff bears the initial
burden of demonstrating that she is the less monied spouse; the burden then
shifts to Defendant to rebut the presumption that fees shall be awarded.

DRL § 237(d) extends the same authority to litigation "expenses," expressly
enumerating appraisal fees among the categories of expenses the court may
direct one spouse to pay — a legislative recognition that in cases involving a
business, the valuation is not an optional luxury but a precondition of
equitable distribution.

The purpose of both provisions, as the Court of Appeals has explained, is to
redress the economic disparity between the monied and the non-monied spouse
and to ensure that the matrimonial scales of justice are not unbalanced by the
weight of the wealthier litigant's wallet. *O'Shea v. O'Shea*, 93 N.Y.2d 187,
190 (1999). The award is committed to the sound discretion of the
trial court, exercised in light of that leveling purpose. *O'Shea*, 93 N.Y.2d
at 193. Procedurally, 22 NYCRR 202.16(k) conditions a fee
application on a sworn statement of net worth and documentation of the fee
arrangement; Plaintiff's compliance, including her candid explanation of why
no retainer agreement yet exists, is set out in the Affirmation at ¶¶ 14–15.

### POINT I

**DEFENDANT IS THE MONIED SPOUSE: HIS OWN PRODUCED RETURNS SHOW INCOME
ROUGHLY SEVEN TIMES PLAINTIFF'S, AND UNDER *DeCABRERA* THE PARTIES' RELATIVE
CIRCUMSTANCES — NOT PLAINTIFF'S INDIGENCY — CONTROL.**

The statutory comparison is straightforward on this record, and every figure
comes from Defendant's side of the discovery ledger.

Defendant's K-1 income over the last three tax years was $175,000 (2022),
$212,000 (2023), and $240,000 (2024) (Exhibits C, D, E) — an average of
$209,000, on a trajectory that rose 37% across the period. Plaintiff's gross
income is $31,200 (Affirmation ¶ 4). The comparison yields:

| Measure | Defendant | Plaintiff | Ratio |
|---------|-----------|-----------|-------|
| Most recent year (2024) | $240,000 | $31,200 | 7.7 : 1 |
| Three-year average | $209,000 | $31,200 | 6.7 : 1 |
| Share of combined 2024 income | 88.5% | 11.5% | — |

The income figures understate the disparity in two respects. First, Defendant
receives perquisites through the corporation — a company truck, phone, and
health coverage (Exhibit E at 2; Affirmation ¶ 8) — that Plaintiff must fund
from her wages. Second, liquidity: Defendant was able to pay a $25,000
retainer from the corporate operating account (Exhibit F at 1–2), while
Plaintiff's total savings are $4,300 (Affirmation ¶ 5).

The anticipated response is that Plaintiff is employed and self-supporting, so
no award is needed. Plaintiff acknowledges the fact squarely: she earns
$31,200 and is not destitute. But the Court of Appeals rejected precisely this
argument as a legal matter in *DeCabrera v. Cabrera-Rosete*, 70 N.Y.2d 879,
881 (1987): indigency is not a prerequisite to a fee award, and the
statute directs the court to the relative circumstances of the parties. The
question is not whether Plaintiff can feed herself; it is whether she can
litigate against a spouse with seven times her income on anything approaching
equal terms. *DeCabrera* answers that an employed spouse earning a modest wage
remains entitled to fees where the disparity is real; here the disparity is
documented by Defendant's own returns. On these relative circumstances,
Defendant is the monied spouse, and the burden passes to him to rebut the
presumption that fees shall be awarded.

### POINT II

**THE COURT SHOULD AWARD INTERIM COUNSEL FEES NOW, NOT AT TRIAL, BECAUSE
DEFERRAL WOULD PERMIT THE MONIED SPOUSE TO LITIGATE THIS CASE BY ATTRITION.**

Defendant may urge that any fee award abide the trial, where the full
financial picture — including the business valuation — will be before the
Court. The Appellate Division has considered and rejected that sequencing. An
interim fee application should not be deferred to the trial court's final
determination: prompt interim awards are what prevent the monied spouse from
wearing down the non-monied spouse through the sheer cost of continuing, and
courts should not relegate the application to a deferred ruling that arrives
only after the damage of unequal representation is done. *Prichep v.
Prichep*, 52 A.D.3d 61, 65–66 (2d Dept 2008).

*Prichep*'s reasoning maps onto this record with little translation. There, as
here, the monied spouse had ready access to funds for counsel while the
non-monied spouse's application was left to await trial; the court held that
approach inconsistent with the statute's leveling purpose. Here the attrition
mechanism is already visible in the papers: Plaintiff has been quoted
retainers of $10,000 and $15,000 (Exhibit G at 1–2), cannot pay either from
$4,300 in savings, and therefore appears pro se against experienced retained
counsel in an action whose central issue — the valuation of a closely held
S-corporation with $1.9 million in revenue — is precisely the kind of issue on
which representation matters most. Every month of deferral is a month in which
one side litigates with counsel paid from a corporate account and the other
side does not litigate so much as endure.

The amount requested is calibrated to the record rather than rounded upward:
$15,000 is the top of the range actually quoted to Plaintiff (Exhibit G at 2),
requested so that she is not forced back to court if the lower quote proves
unavailable, and the application is expressly without prejudice to further
applications — so the Court retains full control over the total, consistent
with the discretion *O'Shea* confirms. 93 N.Y.2d at 193.

### POINT III

**DEFENDANT SHOULD BE DIRECTED TO ADVANCE 100% OF THE NEUTRAL APPRAISER'S FEE
UNDER DRL § 237(d), SUBJECT TO REALLOCATION IN THE FINAL JUDGMENT, BECAUSE AN
EQUAL UP-FRONT SPLIT WOULD PRICE PLAINTIFF OUT OF THE VALUATION BOTH PARTIES
AGREE IS NECESSARY.**

Both parties agree a neutral appraisal of Sample Mechanical Corp. is
appropriate; the neutral has quoted a $12,500 retainer (Exhibit B at 1). The
only dispute is who funds it now.

DRL § 237(d) speaks directly to this expense: appraisal fees are among the
enumerated litigation expenses the court may direct a spouse to pay. The
provision exists because valuation costs are structurally different from
ordinary litigation costs — they are a gate. Without the appraisal there is no
equitable distribution of the principal marital asset; a party who cannot fund
her share of the gate fee is not merely disadvantaged but excluded.

Defendant's counsel's letter of February 9, 2026 (Exhibit A at 1) takes the
position that neutral costs are customarily shared equally. The position is
not frivolous — an equal split of a jointly retained neutral's fee is a
reasonable default *between parties of comparable means*, and Plaintiff does
not suggest the letter was written in bad faith. But the default fails at
exactly the point this record presents: $6,250 exceeds Plaintiff's entire
savings of $4,300 (Affirmation ¶ 12), while Defendant — whose corporation
generated the $1.9 million in revenue being valued (Exhibit C at 1) — funded
his own $25,000 retainer from the corporate account without apparent strain
(Exhibit F at 1–2). Where the disparity in resources is this pronounced, its
practical effect is on the non-monied spouse's very ability to litigate, and
the fee allocation should follow the disparity rather than a formal equality
that operates unequally. *Charpié v. Charpié*, 271 A.D.2d 169, 171–172
(1st Dept 2000).

The relief is framed to protect Defendant as well. Plaintiff seeks an
*advance*, not a final allocation: the order proposed directs the advance
"subject to reallocation in the final judgment," so that if the completed
valuation or the ultimate distribution shows that Plaintiff can and should
bear a share of the appraisal cost, the Court may charge it against her
distributive award. Defendant's remedy for any overpayment is thus built into
the order itself; Plaintiff's exclusion from the valuation, by contrast, could
not be remedied at all.

### Conclusion

Plaintiff respectfully requests an order (1) determining Defendant to be the
monied spouse; (2) awarding Plaintiff $15,000 in interim counsel fees under
DRL § 237(a), without prejudice to further applications; (3) directing
Defendant to advance 100% of the neutral appraiser's $12,500 retainer under
DRL § 237(d), subject to reallocation in the final judgment; and (4) granting
such other and further relief as the Court deems just and proper.

## STATEMENT OF LIMITATIONS

*Self-assessment accompanying this motion, prepared by the drafter.*

1. **The award is discretionary, and the strongest ground for denial is
timing and amount, not entitlement.** DRL § 237(a) creates a presumption, not
a mandate, and the amount and timing remain committed to the Court's
discretion (*O'Shea*, 93 N.Y.2d at 193). The Court could find
$15,000 premature before any appearance by retained counsel. The motion
addresses this by requesting the documented quote range rather than a
projected litigation budget, and by framing the award as without prejudice —
but the vulnerability is real and is acknowledged.

2. **Every income figure comes from the three produced returns; nothing has
been independently verified.** The K-1 figures (Exhibits C–E) are Defendant's
own reported income, and the $1.9 million revenue figure is taken from the
corporate return. If the returns understate or overstate true economic income
— a question the neutral appraisal itself may illuminate — the disparity
analysis in Point I would need to be revisited. The motion's honest position
is that the produced record is the only record, and on that record the
disparity is unambiguous.

3. **Opposing counsel's best counterargument — that an employed spouse needs
no award — is acknowledged, not evaded.** Plaintiff's $31,200 income is stated
in the Affirmation (¶ 4) and in Point I's table rather than minimized. The
answer is legal (*DeCabrera*'s relative-circumstances rule), and its strength
depends on the Court accepting that a 7:1 disparity satisfies the standard;
the papers concede no fact and preserve the entitlement argument for appeal if
the motion is denied.

4. **The § 237(d) advance is provisional by design, and the completed
valuation may alter the final allocation.** The proposed order's reallocation
clause means the ultimate incidence of the $12,500 appraisal cost is decided
at judgment, not now. If the valuation yields a substantial distributive award
to Plaintiff, some or all of the advance may fairly be charged back to her.

5. **Filing judgment and alternatives.** Negotiating a staged payment of the
appraiser's retainer, or deferring the fee application to trial, were both
considered and rejected: the first founders on the same liquidity problem the
motion documents, and the second is foreclosed by *Prichep*'s anti-attrition
rationale. Because Plaintiff is the under-resourced party, the motion itself
is the access-to-justice calibration; the relief asked of Defendant is
proportionate to income he has himself disclosed.
