7. I have not been able to retain counsel. I consulted two matrimonial

## MEMORANDUM OF LAW

### Preliminary Statement

Plaintiff moves under Domestic Relations Law § 237(a) and (d) for interim
counsel fees of $15,000 and for an order directing Defendant to advance the
full fee of the neutral business appraiser. The motion rests on the
substantial disparity between the parties' financial circumstances.

### POINT I — The Parties' Finances

Defendant is the monied spouse. He is the sole shareholder of Sample
Mechanical Corp., which grosses approximately $1.9 million per year, and his
K-1 income over the last three tax years rose from $175,000 to $212,000 to
$240,000. He receives a company truck, phone, and health coverage through the
corporation, and he paid his attorneys a $25,000 retainer from the corporate
operating account. Plaintiff, by contrast, earns $31,200 per year as a
receptionist, has $4,300 in savings, and has no retirement assets in her
name. On these facts the Court should determine that Defendant is the monied
spouse for purposes of this action.

### POINT II — Counsel Fees

Domestic Relations Law § 237(a) establishes a rebuttable presumption that
counsel fees shall be awarded to the less monied spouse. The purpose of the
statute is to redress the economic disparity between spouses and to ensure
that the outcome of a matrimonial case is not shaped by which party can
better afford to litigate. *O'Shea v. O'Shea*, 93 N.Y.2d 187 (1999). The
award rests in the sound discretion of the trial court.

The presumption applies directly here. Defendant's most recent annual income
is roughly eight times Plaintiff's, and Plaintiff cannot meet the retainers
quoted to her ($10,000 to $15,000) from savings of $4,300. There is no
apparent basis on which the presumption could be rebutted.

The award should also be made now, not at the end of the case. An interim
award is the only kind that can serve the statute's purpose: a fee awarded
after trial cannot pay for the lawyer Plaintiff needs today, and requiring
Plaintiff to litigate the valuation of a $1.9 million business without
counsel would create exactly the imbalance the statute is meant to prevent.
The requested $15,000 corresponds to the retainers actually quoted and is
sought without prejudice to further applications.

### POINT III — The Appraisal Fee

Domestic Relations Law § 237(d) defines the expenses the Court may award to
include accountant, appraisal, actuarial, and investigative fees. The neutral
appraiser the parties agree upon has quoted a $12,500 retainer, and
Defendant's counsel has demanded that Plaintiff pay half up front — a demand
Plaintiff simply cannot meet.

Common sense supplies the allocation. The asset to be valued is Defendant's
own business; the party with the income to fund the valuation is Defendant;
and the valuation cannot go forward at all if it must wait on a contribution
from a spouse whose entire savings are less than the demanded half share.
The Court should direct Defendant to advance one hundred percent of the
appraiser's fee, subject to reallocation in the final judgment.

### Conclusion

The motion should be granted in all respects.
